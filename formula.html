Formula

Proven

Bayesian ranking

A raw star rating alone is unreliable. A brand-new restaurant with three five-star reviews looks better than a beloved neighbourhood spot with 4.4 stars across 8,000 visits — but it isn't. Proven uses a Bayesian ranking formula to balance a place's actual rating against the weight of evidence behind it.

score = (C × m + R × v) / (m + v)

C Mean rating of all qualifying results in this search — the prior belief about what an average restaurant looks like here
m 1,000 — the minimum review threshold and prior weight; every place is treated as though it starts with 1,000 imaginary average-rated reviews
R The individual place's actual star rating
v The individual place's real review count

What it does

The formula pulls every place's score toward the search average before sorting. A restaurant with very few reviews gets pulled strongly toward the mean — its score mostly reflects what a typical place here looks like, not its own limited data. A restaurant with thousands of reviews resists that pull; its score stays close to its actual rating because the evidence is strong enough to trust. The more votes a place has earned, the more it speaks for itself.

Example

Assuming a search where the mean rating of all qualifying results is C = 4.2. (C varies with every search — this is illustrative.)

Corner Slice

4.8 stars  ·  80 reviews
score = (4.2 × 1,000 + 4.8 × 80) / (1,000 + 80)
= 4,584 / 1,080 ≈ 4.24

4.24

Proven score

Joe's Diner

4.4 stars  ·  5,200 reviews
score = (4.2 × 1,000 + 4.4 × 5,200) / (1,000 + 5,200)
= 27,080 / 6,200 ≈ 4.37

4.37

Proven score

Joe's Diner ranks higher despite its lower raw rating. Corner Slice's 4.8 stars comes from only 80 people — impressive, but statistically thin. Joe's 4.4 from 5,200 visits is a verdict delivered by thousands of independent decisions. Proven surfaces the place the city has actually chosen, not the one that got lucky with a small sample.
